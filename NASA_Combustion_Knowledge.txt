In the United States, combustion processes contribute to about 85% of delivered energy and are an integral part in many industrial fabrication processes. Combustion produces greenhouse gases and soot, which contributes to global warming and causes significant health concerns. The Combustion Science Discipline conducts idealized experiments on the International Space Station where the removal of gravity enables researchers to study details of the combustion processes that cannot be readily studied on the ground. The Space Station environment also provides an important test-bed for studying spacecraft fire risk.

Combustion occurs when fuel and oxygen react to produce carbon dioxide, water and heat. For the foreseeable future, the overwhelming majority of delivered energy in terrestrial applications will be from combustion or other chemically reacting systems. These energy uses cover the range from electric power and transportation to processes directly tied to the delivered material (e.g., glass and steel manufacture). These processes produce some of the most important environmental hazards currently facing humanity (global climate change, acid gas pollution, mercury contamination from coal, and wild-land fires).

Despite being the subject of active research for over 80 years, combustion processes remain one of the most poorly controlled phenomena that have a significant impact on human health, comfort and safety. This is because the simplest combustor (e.g., kitchen stove) remains beyond our detailed numerical modeling capabilities. Typically, the combustion process involves a large number of chemical species (hundreds) and reactions (even thousands). It is these species and reactions that determine flammability limits (combustor operating ranges) and pollutant emissions. Much of combustion research involves developing a comprehensive and predictive quantitative understanding of this complex process. This understanding usually occurs in smaller, bench-scale experiments that are amenable to detailed study and modeling. In these bench-scale experiments, however, buoyant forces, created by gravity and the large temperature differential between the flame and the ambient gas, dominate and frequently obscure the physical and chemical phenomena of interest, and hence, complicate analyses. In the absence of gravity, buoyancy can be suppressed, and the analyses can be reduced to much simpler one-dimensional systems.

To understand the strong influence of gravity on flames, one can consider the density gradients in flames. Typical flame temperatures are on the order of 2,300 degrees Kelvin, whereas ambient temperatures are approximately 300 degrees Kelvin. This produces an eight-fold density change over the scale of a centimeter. The resultant density gradient induces a strong velocity field that dominates all but the highest flow fields. This flow field causes the flame to lift and point upward. The resultant flow instabilities cause the flicker typical of flames on Earth. FM2 is the first-ever combustion experiment to be performed on another planetary body. Building on the success of the decades-long combustion science ground (1g) program and International Space Station (ISS) microgravity (~0g) combustion experiment campaigns, FM2 investigates burning materials on the Moon at its 1/6 reduced gravity level. Previous computational modeling, bolstered by experiments aboard parabolicflight aircraft and the ISS, suggest that materials may burn at lower oxygen concentrations in lunar gravity compared to Earth. This means that some things that are not flammable on Earth, may be flammable in potential habitats on the Moon and other places with reduced gravity. Since fire can be a catastrophic hazard for spaceflight and extreme human exploration, FM2 is a critical step in determining material flammability and safety for future missions.

President John F. Kennedy’s goal of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth by the end of the decade led NASA to set ambitious goals for 1967. The agency aimed to human-rate the Saturn IB and Saturn V rockets, as well as test the Apollo Command and Service Modules (CSM) and Lunar Module. Apollo I was the first of these missions and would be a 14-day Earth orbital test flight of a Block I Apollo CSM. The primary objectives included testing the spacecraft’s life support systems and the main Service Propulsion System (SPS) engine, which would be used for critical maneuvers on lunar missions. The mission plan included eight SPS test firings, with the final firing intended to deorbit the spacecraft for reentry during its 208th revolution around the Earth.

North American Aviation planned to build two versions of Apollo that could carry a crew, with astronauts using the more basic Block I version to test the spacecraft in Earth orbit. A Block II version would be equipped with a docking station and take astronauts to the Moon. Following two uncrewed suborbital tests, AS-204, later to be known as Apollo I, would have become the first Block I to carry astronauts into Earth orbit.

The agency assigned spaceflight veterans Virgil “Gus” Grissom and Edward White to the mission, along with spaceflight rookie Roger Chaffee. NASA announced the crew in a press conference held on March 21, 1966.

Grissom, White and Chaffee began training for the mission several months before being announced, spending considerable time with their spacecraft while it was under construction at North American Aviation and taking part in classroom lectures. They also trained in Apollo Command Module (CM) simulators at the Manned Spacecraft Center (now Johnson Space Center) and Kennedy Space Center.

On the first day of the mission, the crew planned to fly in formation with the rocket’s S-IVB second stage for 20 minutes to photograph its condition. They also aimed to spend about 60 hours performing nine experiments, including five medical, three scientific and one technological. Additionally, they planned one television broadcast, which would’ve been the first to be beamed down from an American-crewed spacecraft in orbit.

If the mission lasted its full duration, the spacecraft would bring the astronauts home to a splashdown in the Atlantic Ocean after 330 hours and 50 minutes, breaking Gemini VII’s record and making it the longest human spaceflight.

Preparations for follow-on missions were already underway in January 1967, and if all missions accomplished their objectives, NASA hoped a Moon landing might be achievable by late 1968. With spacecraft and rocket stage delivery delays, these lofty goals were already threatened before the tragic events that followed.

Many viewed the Apollo 13 mission as “routine” after the overwhelming successes of the Apollo 11 and Apollo 12 missions, but things did not go as planned.

Misfortune plagued the mission early on, with ground tests before launch indicating the possibility of a poorly insulated helium tank in the Lunar Module and the previously damaged oxygen tank number two.

Days before the mission, Charles Duke accidentally exposed the crew to German measles and as a precaution, Jack Swigert replaced Command Module Pilot Ken Mattingly, who lacked immunity.

On launch day, 5 ½ minutes after liftoff, the center engine of the S-II shut down two minutes early, causing the remaining four engines to burn 34 seconds longer than planned and the S-IVB third stage to burn nine seconds longer to put Apollo 13 in orbit.

Things went smoothly until the third day of the mission when, after finishing a 49-minute TV broadcast showing the conditions on board the spacecraft, oxygen tank number two blew up, leading to the subsequent failure of tank number one.

The option for a lunar landing swiftly disappeared and the astronauts and mission control worked tirelessly around the clock to develop new procedures to get the crew home safely in a race against time.
On June 15, 1970, Apollo 13 Review Board Chair Edgar M. Cortright released the final report detailing the cause of the oxygen tank explosion. The report found that the accident was a combination of mistakes and a deficient design. The board conducted seven weeks of investigation with four panels looking into different aspects of the accident.
On June 16, 1970, Edgar Cortright summarized the findings in a hearing before the House Committee on Science and Aeronautics and informed them of the Review Board’s recommendations, which included the redesign and recertification of the Service Module oxygen storage system and a thorough review by NASA of all Apollo systems that use cryogenic oxygen. The Review Board also recommended that management and organizational structures be strengthened to ensure an adequate understanding of the engineering and manufacturing processes of subsystems.

For Apollo 14 and future missions, engineers at NASA and North American Rockwell redesigned the oxygen tank system by adding a third tank in a different bay, upgrading thermostats, removing stirring fans and sheathing all electrical wiring in stainless steel.

The International Space Station provides a unique microgravity environment that allows researchers to study fundamental combustion processes by eliminating the complex interactions between buoyant flows and energy feedback to the flame that dominate terrestrial experiments. In normal gravity, the large temperature differences between a flame and the ambient gas create strong density gradients that induce velocity fields, causing flames to lift, flicker, and obscure underlying physical and chemical phenomena. By conducting experiments in microgravity, researchers can isolate these variables, enabling the study of nearly one-dimensional systems, such as the spherically symmetric combustion of liquid droplets in a quiescent atmosphere. These droplet combustion studies, conducted using facilities like the Combustion Integrated Rack and the Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus, have been crucial in validating physical and chemical kinetic sub-models used for practical fuels on Earth. The Flame Extinguishment Experiment (FLEX) and its successor, FLEX-2, have specifically investigated the flammability limits of fuels like heptane and methanol when mixed with inert gas suppressants. These experiments have yielded unexpected results, demonstrating that flammability limits are lower in reduced gravity, meaning that terrestrial standards for inert gas suppressants do not offer the same margin of safety in space. Furthermore, FLEX experiments led to the discovery of stable, quasi-steady cool flames that continue to burn after the visible hot flame has radiatively extinguished. This low-temperature chemistry is highly relevant to developing next-generation, high-efficiency internal combustion engines on Earth. Beyond droplet combustion, the ISS has hosted numerous experiments on gaseous diffusion flames, focusing on soot processes, flame stability, and extinction limits. The Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project, operating within the Combustion Integrated Rack, encompasses several investigations such as the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame experiment, which studies soot formation in highly diluted and sooty flames, and the Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames experiment, which investigates how electric fields can control non-premixed flames by manipulating flame ions. Solid-fuel combustion and material flammability are also critical areas of study, primarily driven by the need for spacecraft fire safety. Microgravity experiments, such as the Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS) investigation conducted in the Microgravity Science Glovebox, have shown that in low-flow conditions, materials can ignite more easily and burn at lower oxygen concentrations than in normal gravity due to reduced convective cooling. Counter-intuitively, flames in low-speed airflows in microgravity may preferentially spread upstream into the oncoming airflow. These findings indicate that current terrestrial flammability tests, such as NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, may not be conservative enough for spacecraft environments, necessitating the development of new testing methodologies like the Narrow Channel test. Future solid fuel research will be supported by the Solid Fuel Ignition and Extinction (SoFIE) hardware, which aims to characterize ignition and flammability in practical geometries and realistic Space Exploration Atmospheres with reduced pressure and elevated oxygen. Premixed flames have also been studied, leading to the discovery of stable flame balls at low Lewis numbers, a phenomenon only possible in microgravity, which has improved the understanding of combustion reactions near extinction limits. Additionally, the ISS supports research on super-critical processes, such as Super-Critical Water Oxidation, which has applications for closed-loop life-support systems and waste management. Experiments have shown that the lack of buoyant mixing in microgravity leads to thermal stratification in super-critical water reactors. The success of these varied combustion experiments relies on specialized ISS facilities, including the Combustion Integrated Rack, which provides a 100-liter chamber with advanced diagnostics and gas distribution, and the Microgravity Science Glovebox, which offers a large enclosed working volume with power, cooling, and vacuum resources for a wide range of investigations. Accelerometers like the Space Acceleration Measurement System and the Microgravity Acceleration Measurement System continuously monitor the vibratory and quasi-steady acceleration environment to ensure the integrity of the scientific data collected in these unique microgravity conditions.

Cool flames represent a distinct low-temperature combustion regime that plays a critical role in ignition timing, burning rates, flammability limits, and engine knock phenomena in advanced internal combustion engines. Unlike conventional hot flames, which operate at high temperatures (typically above 1500 K) and are governed by hydrogen-oxygen chain-branching chemistry, cool flames operate in a significantly lower temperature range (typically 500-800 K) and are driven by peroxy chain-branching pathways. A key characteristic of cool flame chemistry is the Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) behavior, a kinetic phenomenon where the overall reaction rate and heat release paradoxically decrease as the mixture temperature increases. This occurs because the primary low-temperature chain-branching pathway, which involves the formation of alkylperoxy radicals (RO2), hydroperoxyl alkyl radicals (QOOH), and ketohydroperoxides (OQ'OOH), is suppressed at higher temperatures. Specifically, as the temperature rises, the equilibrium of oxygen addition reactions shifts toward the reactants, and the competitive chain-propagating decomposition of QOOH to form cyclic ethers and OH radicals becomes dominant, thereby reducing degenerate branching and slowing down the overall radical production. The NTC region typically falls between 750 K and 1000 K, acting as a kinetic boundary that can lead to thermo-kinetic instabilities, oscillatory burning, and multi-stage ignition. The occurrence of cool flames significantly extends the flammability limits of both premixed and non-premixed mixtures beyond the classical hot flame extinction limits. In fuel-lean premixed conditions, the lower flammability limit of a cool flame occurs at much lower equivalence ratios than that of a hot flame. Furthermore, at elevated pressures, the transition from a cool flame to a hot flame can proceed smoothly through a two-stage warm flame structure, avoiding abrupt extinction. Recent microgravity droplet combustion experiments, notably the Flame Extinguishment (FLEX) project on the International Space Station, have conclusively demonstrated that large n-alkane droplets can burn quasi-steadily in a purely non-premixed cool flame mode. After the visible hot flame radiatively extinguishes due to excessive heat loss at low Damkohler numbers, the droplet continues to vaporize, supported solely by a lower-temperature, invisible cool flame. This cool flame burning mode exhibits significantly different extinction limits and dynamics compared to hot flames. In environments with low oxygen indices, near the Limiting Oxygen Index (LOI), a steady cool flame cannot be sustained; instead, the combustion transitions to a dynamically oscillating mode. This oscillatory cool flame behavior is characterized by periodic fluctuations in the flame stand-off ratio, peak gas temperature, and burning rate. The physical mechanism behind these oscillations involves the dynamic depletion and subsequent re-establishment of the fuel vapor and oxidizer layer near the droplet surface. Kinetically, the oscillations are driven by the dynamic competition between the degenerate chain branching of QOOH and the chain termination/propagation pathways, causing the local conditions to repeatedly cross the NTC turnover temperature. During each cycle, the flame expands outward as local oxygen is depleted, allowing unreacted oxygen to diffuse back into the reaction zone, which then triggers a temperature rise, flame contraction, and a subsequent decrease in reactivity due to the NTC effect. As the oscillatory burning progresses, an increasing amount of unreacted fuel vapor and intermediate species leak through the partially oxidized cool flame zone and accumulate in the far-field, leading to an ever-increasing amplitude of the oscillations until the liquid fuel is completely depleted. These findings highlight the critical importance of incorporating detailed low-temperature chemistry and transport coupling into predictive computational models, as conventional high-temperature models fail to capture the extended flammability limits, oscillatory dynamics, and unique burning regimes introduced by cool flames in both microgravity environments and advanced terrestrial engines.

Inverse Diffusion Flames (IDF) in Microgravity:
Unlike standard diffusion flames where fuel is injected into an oxidizer environment, Inverse Diffusion Flames inject an oxidizer into a fuel-rich environment. NASA studies IDF in microgravity primarily to understand soot formation and carbon particulate emissions. In normal gravity, soot particles pass through the flame front and oxidize, making it difficult to study their formation. In an IDF configuration, soot forms on the outside of the flame and escapes without oxidizing, allowing researchers to measure soot precursors cleanly. In microgravity, the lack of buoyancy prevents the flame from flickering or stretching, creating a highly stable, broader flame structure perfect for observing soot concentration and temperature profiles. Understanding soot is critical for spacecraft fire safety, as unburned carbon particulates can quickly clog life support filters and pose severe toxic hazards to astronauts.
